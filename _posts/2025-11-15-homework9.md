## Introduction

This homework explores the foundational aspects of probability theory, examining how different interpretations of probability have evolved and how the axiomatic approach provides a unified mathematical framework. We will investigate the deep connection between probability theory and measure theory, and derive fundamental properties from the axioms of probability.

## Part 1: Interpretations of Probability

### 1.1 Classical (Laplacian) Interpretation

**Definition:** The probability of an event A is the ratio of favorable outcomes to total possible outcomes, assuming all outcomes are equally likely.

```
P(A) = |A| / |Ω| = (Number of favorable outcomes) / (Total number of outcomes)
```

**Example:** Rolling a fair die, P(even number) = 3/6 = 0.5

**Strengths:**
- Intuitive and easy to apply in simple cases
- Provides exact calculations for finite, symmetric sample spaces
- Foundation for combinatorial probability

**Limitations:**
- Requires outcomes to be equally likely (circular reasoning)
- Cannot handle continuous sample spaces
- Fails for infinite sample spaces
- Cannot address cases where symmetry is not obvious

**Historical Context:** Proposed by Pierre-Simon Laplace in the 18th century, this was the first systematic approach to probability.

### 1.2 Frequentist Interpretation

**Definition:** The probability of an event is the limiting relative frequency of its occurrence in an infinite sequence of independent trials.

```
P(A) = lim_{n→∞} (n_A / n)
```

where n_A is the number of times event A occurs in n trials.

**Example:** If we flip a coin 10,000 times and observe 5,023 heads, P(heads) ≈ 0.5023

**Strengths:**
- Based on empirical observation
- Applicable to real-world experiments
- Provides operational meaning to probability
- No need to assume equally likely outcomes

**Limitations:**
- Requires repeatable experiments
- Cannot handle one-time events (e.g., "probability of rain tomorrow")
- The limit may not exist in practice
- Infinite repetition is physically impossible

**Historical Context:** Developed by Richard von Mises and others in the early 20th century as a response to the limitations of the classical approach.

### 1.3 Bayesian (Subjective) Interpretation

**Definition:** Probability represents a degree of belief or confidence about an event, updated through experience using Bayes' theorem.

```
P(H|E) = P(E|H) · P(H) / P(E)
```

**Key Concepts:**
- **Prior probability P(H):** Initial belief before observing evidence
- **Likelihood P(E|H):** Probability of evidence given hypothesis
- **Posterior probability P(H|E):** Updated belief after observing evidence

**Example:** Estimating the probability that a patient has a disease given test results

**Strengths:**
- Handles one-time events and subjective uncertainties
- Incorporates prior knowledge
- Natural framework for updating beliefs with new information
- Applicable to decision-making under uncertainty

**Limitations:**
- Different individuals may assign different prior probabilities
- Subjective nature can lead to disagreements
- Choice of prior can significantly influence results
- Requires careful elicitation of beliefs

**Betting Mechanism:** To quantify subjective probability, consider: "How much would you be willing to bet to receive $100 if event A occurs?" If you'd bet $p, then your subjective probability is P(A) = p/100. Additionally, you should be willing to take either side of the bet at these odds.

**Historical Context:** Foundations laid by Thomas Bayes (18th century), developed extensively by Bruno de Finetti and Leonard Savage in the 20th century.

### 1.4 Geometric (Continuous) Interpretation

**Definition:** For continuous sample spaces, probability is determined by geometric measures (length, area, volume).

```
P(A) = measure(A) / measure(Ω)
```

**Example:** Probability that a randomly chosen point in a unit square falls in a specific region

**Strengths:**
- Extends probability to continuous spaces
- Natural for physical problems (e.g., particle positions)
- Foundation for continuous random variables

**Limitations:**
- Requires careful definition of "random" selection
- Some sets may not be measurable (Vitali set)
- Paradoxes can arise (Bertrand's paradox)

### 1.5 Conceptual Inconsistencies Resolved by Axiomatic Approach

**Problems Among Interpretations:**

1. **Circular reasoning in classical approach:** Defining probability using "equally likely" outcomes assumes what we're trying to define

2. **Incompatibility:** Frequentist approach cannot handle unique events; Bayesian allows them but is subjective

3. **Infinite sample spaces:** Classical approach breaks down; geometric approach introduces measurability issues

4. **Mathematical rigor:** None of the interpretations provides a rigorous mathematical foundation

**Resolution through Axioms:**

The axiomatic approach, proposed by Andrei Kolmogorov (1933), provides a unified mathematical framework independent of interpretation:

**Kolmogorov's Axioms:**
1. **Non-negativity:** P(A) ≥ 0 for all events A
2. **Normalization:** P(Ω) = 1 (certainty of sample space)
3. **Countable additivity:** For disjoint events A₁, A₂, ...:
   ```
   P(⋃ᵢ Aᵢ) = Σᵢ P(Aᵢ)
   ```

**Why This Resolves Inconsistencies:**

- **Interpretation-independent:** The axioms don't specify *what* probability means, only how it must behave mathematically
- **Mathematical rigor:** Built on measure theory, a solid mathematical foundation
- **Universality:** All interpretations must satisfy these axioms
- **Consistency:** Different interpretations can coexist as different ways to *assign* probabilities, but all must obey the same rules

**Example:** Both a frequentist (using relative frequencies) and a Bayesian (using degrees of belief) can work with the same probability space (Ω, F, P) as long as their probability assignments satisfy the axioms.

## Part 2: Probability Theory and Measure Theory

### 2.1 Measure Theory Foundation

**Measure Space:** A triplet (Ω, F, μ) where:
- **Ω:** Set of all elements (universal set)
- **F:** σ-algebra on Ω (collection of measurable sets)
- **μ:** Measure function, μ: F → [0, ∞]

**Properties of a Measure:**
1. **Non-negativity:** μ(A) ≥ 0 for all A ∈ F
2. **Null empty set:** μ(∅) = 0
3. **Countable additivity:** For disjoint sets A₁, A₂, ...:
   ```
   μ(⋃ᵢ Aᵢ) = Σᵢ μ(Aᵢ)
   ```

### 2.2 σ-Algebras (Sigma-Algebras)

**Definition:** A σ-algebra F on a set Ω is a collection of subsets satisfying:

1. **Contains Ω:** Ω ∈ F
2. **Closed under complementation:** If A ∈ F, then Aᶜ ∈ F
3. **Closed under countable unions:** If A₁, A₂, ... ∈ F, then ⋃ᵢ Aᵢ ∈ F

**Consequences:**
- ∅ ∈ F (since ∅ = Ωᶜ)
- Closed under countable intersections (by De Morgan's laws)
- Closed under set differences

**Why σ-Algebras?**

σ-algebras define which subsets are "measurable" (i.e., which events can be assigned probabilities). Not all subsets can be measurable without leading to contradictions (e.g., Vitali sets in ℝ).

**Example:** For Ω = {1, 2, 3}, one possible σ-algebra is:
```
F = {∅, {1}, {2,3}, {1,2,3}}
```

**Common σ-Algebras:**
- **Power set:** F = P(Ω), all subsets (for finite/countable Ω)
- **Borel σ-algebra:** Generated by open sets in ℝⁿ (for continuous spaces)
- **Trivial σ-algebra:** F = {∅, Ω}

### 2.3 Probability Measures

**Definition:** A probability measure P is a measure on (Ω, F) such that:
```
P(Ω) = 1
```

Thus, a **probability space** is a measure space (Ω, F, P) where P is a probability measure.

**Key Difference from General Measures:**
- General measure: μ(Ω) can be ∞ (e.g., Lebesgue measure on ℝ)
- Probability measure: P(Ω) = 1 (normalized)

**Interpretation:**
- **Ω:** Sample space (all possible outcomes)
- **F:** Event space (all events we can assign probabilities to)
- **P:** Probability measure (assigns probabilities to events)

### 2.4 Measurable Functions and Random Variables

**Measurable Function:** A function f: (Ω, F) → (S, Σ) is measurable if:
```
f⁻¹(B) ∈ F  for all B ∈ Σ
```

i.e., the preimage of every measurable set in S is measurable in Ω.

**Random Variable:** A measurable function X: (Ω, F, P) → (ℝ, B, P_X) where:
- **Ω:** Sample space
- **F:** σ-algebra of events
- **P:** Probability measure on (Ω, F)
- **B:** Borel σ-algebra on ℝ
- **P_X:** Induced probability measure on ℝ (distribution of X)

**Why This Definition?**

For X to be a random variable, we need to be able to calculate probabilities like P(X ∈ B) for any Borel set B. This requires:
```
{ω ∈ Ω : X(ω) ∈ B} ∈ F
```

i.e., X⁻¹(B) must be an event in our probability space.

**Example from Random Walk:**

In Homework 7, each step Xᵢ is a random variable:
```
Xᵢ: Ω → {-1, +1}
```

where Ω represents all possible outcomes of week i (secure or breached), and:
- Xᵢ⁻¹({+1}) = {server secure} ∈ F
- Xᵢ⁻¹({-1}) = {server breached} ∈ F

The cumulative score S(n) = X₁ + X₂ + ... + Xₙ is also a random variable.

### 2.5 Summary of Connections

| Measure Theory | Probability Theory |
|----------------|-------------------|
| Measure space (Ω, F, μ) | Probability space (Ω, F, P) |
| Measure μ | Probability measure P |
| μ(Ω) ∈ [0, ∞] | P(Ω) = 1 |
| Measurable set A ∈ F | Event A |
| Measurable function | Random variable |
| μ(A) | P(A) |

**Key Insight:** Probability theory is measure theory with the additional constraint that the total measure equals 1. This connection provides probability theory with rigorous mathematical foundations.

## Part 3: Deriving Properties from Axioms

### 3.1 Basic Properties

From Kolmogorov's axioms, we can derive:

**Property 1: Probability of Empty Set**
```
P(∅) = 0
```

*Proof:* Let A₁ = Ω and Aᵢ = ∅ for i > 1. These are disjoint, so:
```
P(Ω) = P(⋃ᵢ Aᵢ) = P(Ω) + P(∅) + P(∅) + ...
1 = 1 + Σᵢ₌₂^∞ P(∅)
```
This is only possible if P(∅) = 0. □

**Property 2: Complement Rule**
```
P(Aᶜ) = 1 - P(A)
```

*Proof:* Since A and Aᶜ are disjoint and A ∪ Aᶜ = Ω:
```
P(Ω) = P(A ∪ Aᶜ) = P(A) + P(Aᶜ)
1 = P(A) + P(Aᶜ)
P(Aᶜ) = 1 - P(A)
```
□

**Property 3: Monotonicity**

If A ⊆ B, then P(A) ≤ P(B)

*Proof:* We can write B = A ∪ (B ∩ Aᶜ), where A and (B ∩ Aᶜ) are disjoint:
```
P(B) = P(A) + P(B ∩ Aᶜ)
```
Since P(B ∩ Aᶜ) ≥ 0 (non-negativity axiom):
```
P(B) ≥ P(A)
```
□

### 3.2 Subadditivity Property

**Theorem (Subadditivity):** For any sequence of events A₁, A₂, A₃, ... (not necessarily disjoint):
```
P(⋃ᵢ₌₁^∞ Aᵢ) ≤ Σᵢ₌₁^∞ P(Aᵢ)
```

For finite case (n events):
```
P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ ... ∪ Aₙ) ≤ P(A₁) + P(A₂) + ... + P(Aₙ)
```

**Proof (Countable Case):**

Define a sequence of disjoint events:
```
B₁ = A₁
B₂ = A₂ ∩ A₁ᶜ
B₃ = A₃ ∩ A₁ᶜ ∩ A₂ᶜ
...
Bₙ = Aₙ ∩ (⋃ᵢ₌₁^(n-1) Aᵢ)ᶜ
```

These events are disjoint by construction, and:
```
⋃ᵢ₌₁^∞ Bᵢ = ⋃ᵢ₌₁^∞ Aᵢ
```

By countable additivity:
```
P(⋃ᵢ₌₁^∞ Aᵢ) = P(⋃ᵢ₌₁^∞ Bᵢ) = Σᵢ₌₁^∞ P(Bᵢ)
```

Since Bᵢ ⊆ Aᵢ for all i, by monotonicity P(Bᵢ) ≤ P(Aᵢ):
```
P(⋃ᵢ₌₁^∞ Aᵢ) = Σᵢ₌₁^∞ P(Bᵢ) ≤ Σᵢ₌₁^∞ P(Aᵢ)
```
□

**Interpretation:** The probability of a union is at most the sum of individual probabilities. Equality holds only when events are disjoint (no overlap).

**Example:** In security analysis, if event Aᵢ represents "breach occurs through vulnerability i", then:
```
P(at least one breach) ≤ P(breach through vuln. 1) + ... + P(breach through vuln. n)
```

This is often called the **union bound** or **Boole's inequality**.

### 3.3 Inclusion-Exclusion Principle

**Theorem (Inclusion-Exclusion):** For any events A₁, A₂, ..., Aₙ:

```
P(⋃ᵢ₌₁ⁿ Aᵢ) = Σᵢ P(Aᵢ) - Σᵢ<ⱼ P(Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ) + Σᵢ<ⱼ<ₖ P(Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ ∩ Aₖ) - ... + (-1)ⁿ⁺¹ P(A₁ ∩ ... ∩ Aₙ)
```

**Proof by Induction:**

*Base case (n=2):*
```
P(A₁ ∪ A₂) = P(A₁) + P(A₂) - P(A₁ ∩ A₂)
```

Proof: Write A₁ ∪ A₂ as disjoint union:
```
A₁ ∪ A₂ = A₁ ∪ (A₂ ∩ A₁ᶜ)
```

Then:
```
P(A₁ ∪ A₂) = P(A₁) + P(A₂ ∩ A₁ᶜ)
```

Also, A₂ = (A₂ ∩ A₁) ∪ (A₂ ∩ A₁ᶜ), so:
```
P(A₂) = P(A₂ ∩ A₁) + P(A₂ ∩ A₁ᶜ)
P(A₂ ∩ A₁ᶜ) = P(A₂) - P(A₁ ∩ A₂)
```

Substituting:
```
P(A₁ ∪ A₂) = P(A₁) + P(A₂) - P(A₁ ∩ A₂)
```
□

*Inductive step:* Assume the formula holds for n events. For n+1 events:

Let B = ⋃ᵢ₌₁ⁿ Aᵢ. Then:
```
P(⋃ᵢ₌₁ⁿ⁺¹ Aᵢ) = P(B ∪ Aₙ₊₁) = P(B) + P(Aₙ₊₁) - P(B ∩ Aₙ₊₁)
```

By inductive hypothesis, P(B) satisfies inclusion-exclusion. Also:
```
B ∩ Aₙ₊₁ = ⋃ᵢ₌₁ⁿ (Aᵢ ∩ Aₙ₊₁)
```

Applying inclusion-exclusion to this union and substituting gives the formula for n+1 events. □

**Alternative Proof (Combinatorial):**

Each element ω ∈ ⋃ᵢ Aᵢ belongs to exactly k ≥ 1 of the sets A₁, ..., Aₙ.

On the right-hand side, this element is counted:
- In Σᵢ P(Aᵢ): k times
- In -Σᵢ<ⱼ P(Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ): -C(k,2) times
- In Σᵢ<ⱼ<ₖ P(Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ ∩ Aₖ): +C(k,3) times
- etc.

Total count:
```
C(k,1) - C(k,2) + C(k,3) - ... + (-1)ᵏ⁺¹C(k,k) = 1 - (1-1)ᵏ = 1
```

(by binomial theorem), which matches the left-hand side. □

**Special Cases:**

For n=3:
```
P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ A₃) = P(A₁) + P(A₂) + P(A₃) 
                   - P(A₁ ∩ A₂) - P(A₁ ∩ A₃) - P(A₂ ∩ A₃)
                   + P(A₁ ∩ A₂ ∩ A₃)
```

**Comparison with Subadditivity:**

Inclusion-exclusion gives an exact formula, while subadditivity gives an upper bound:
```
P(⋃ᵢ Aᵢ) = Σᵢ P(Aᵢ) - [correction terms] ≤ Σᵢ P(Aᵢ)
```

The correction terms account for overlaps (double-counting).

### 3.4 Applications and Examples

**Example 1: Security Systems**

A server has three independent vulnerabilities with exploit probabilities:
- P(A₁) = 0.3
- P(A₂) = 0.2
- P(A₃) = 0.4

What is P(at least one vulnerability exploited)?

*Subadditivity bound:*
```
P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ A₃) ≤ 0.3 + 0.2 + 0.4 = 0.9
```

If independent (P(Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ) = P(Aᵢ)P(Aⱼ)):
```
P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ A₃) = 0.3 + 0.2 + 0.4 
                  - 0.06 - 0.12 - 0.08 
                  + 0.024
                  = 0.664
```

Or equivalently:
```
P(no exploit) = (1-0.3)(1-0.2)(1-0.4) = 0.336
P(at least one) = 1 - 0.336 = 0.664
```

**Example 2: Counting Principles**

How many integers from 1 to 100 are divisible by 2, 3, or 5?

Let:
- A₁ = {divisible by 2}
- A₂ = {divisible by 3}
- A₃ = {divisible by 5}

Then:
```
|A₁| = 50, |A₂| = 33, |A₃| = 20
|A₁ ∩ A₂| = 16, |A₁ ∩ A₃| = 10, |A₂ ∩ A₃| = 6
|A₁ ∩ A₂ ∩ A₃| = 3
```

By inclusion-exclusion:
```
|A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ A₃| = 50 + 33 + 20 - 16 - 10 - 6 + 3 = 74
```

### 3.5 Generalization: Bonferroni Inequalities

The inclusion-exclusion principle leads to alternating upper and lower bounds:

**Lower bounds (odd k):**
```
P(⋃ᵢ Aᵢ) ≥ S₁ - S₂ + S₃ - ... + S₂ₖ₋₁
```

**Upper bounds (even k):**
```
P(⋃ᵢ Aᵢ) ≤ S₁ - S₂ + S₃ - ... - S₂ₖ
```

where Sₖ is the sum of probabilities of all k-way intersections:
```
S₁ = Σᵢ P(Aᵢ)
S₂ = Σᵢ<ⱼ P(Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ)
S₃ = Σᵢ<ⱼ<ₖ P(Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ ∩ Aₖ)
...
```

## Conclusion

This homework has demonstrated:

1. **Multiple interpretations** of probability exist (classical, frequentist, Bayesian, geometric), each with strengths and limitations

2. **The axiomatic approach** provides a unified mathematical framework that resolves conceptual inconsistencies by defining probability as a normalized measure

3. **Measure theory** provides the rigorous foundation for probability theory through:
   - σ-algebras (defining measurable events)
   - Probability measures (assigning probabilities)
   - Measurable functions (random variables)

4. **Fundamental properties** like subadditivity and inclusion-exclusion can be rigorously derived from Kolmogorov's axioms

5. **Practical applications** of these principles appear throughout statistics, from security analysis to combinatorial counting

The connection between probability and measure theory, established by Kolmogorov, transformed probability from a collection of intuitions into a rigorous mathematical discipline.
